  CineBlock: The Original Project

  CineBlock was built at a hackathon using a world model (from World Labs) with the Marvell API. The core idea: upload a few reference images of an environment from    
  different directions, and the tool generates a rough 3D representation of that scene. It's imprecise — artifacts, noise, not photorealistic — but it gives a working  
  approximation of the 3D space. You can drop in mannequin-style characters and basic primitive objects (cubes, cones, etc.), move them around, and use it for basic    
  blocking and framing.

  The Integration Vision

  The idea is to use CineBlock as a shot-listing and blocking tool that feeds into AI video generation. Modern video models support image-to-video: give a start frame, 
  an end frame, or both, and the model generates the in-between frames. The insight is that if you give these models a rough compositional sketch alongside a text      
  prompt and asset attachments (character images, prop images, location images), they're smart enough to interpret the intent and produce a coherent frame — even from a   crude input.

  So the workflow would be: use CineBlock to quickly block out each shot in the 3D space, capture that rough world model still as a reference frame, then pipe that     
  frame + the text prompt + the relevant assets into the video model to generate the actual clip.

  The Problem: Model Confusion Under Information Overload

  The main challenge encountered previously was that when too many assets and reference images were fed in alongside a prompt, the model couldn't prioritize
  intelligently. It tried to satisfy all inputs simultaneously rather than using context to eliminate irrelevant information. Example: an over-the-shoulder shot of     
  SpongeBob grabbing Squidward's shirt should only show SpongeBob's back and Squidward's face — but the model would default to a generic medium shot with both
  characters facing forward, because it couldn't reconcile the text prompt with the wide-angle character and location reference images it was also given.

  A related issue: location asset images are often wide, high-angle, or composed differently than the intended shot. The model needs to understand that the world model 
  still defines the framing and composition, while the location asset just informs the visual style and content of the background — not the angle or crop.

  The Asset Continuity Problem

  Scenes change mid-scene: a character gets bloodied, changes clothes, moves to a different room. Manually supplying new precise asset images for every such variation, 
  for every shot, becomes extremely tedious and eliminates most of the time-saving benefit. This is a real bottleneck that needs to be addressed — ideally the system   
  handles these variations somewhat autonomously, guided by the text prompt rather than requiring manual per-shot asset updates.

  Product Positioning

  CineBlock is not trying to compete with professional tools like Adobe Creative Suite — that's a different market, a much bigger team, and there's no real
  differentiation space there. On the other end, pure AI video wrappers (VO3, Kling, etc.) basically just let the model do everything, producing "slop" — sometimes     
  cohesive but never precise or intentional.

  The target is the happy middle: give creative people enough control to produce something that actually reflects their vision, without requiring them to learn
  professional animation or editing tools. The target user is someone like a 20-something content creator — a part-writer type who spends most of their time on the     
  story and jokes, and just needs a fast pipeline to produce it visually. Think: someone who wants to turn around a parody video the same day they see a news event, or 
  a YouTube/Twitter creator making scripted animated content. The goal is quick turnaround, not perfect continuity — but also not slop.

  The Core Bet

  If CineBlock's world model stills can serve as reliable compositional references for each shot, and if the underlying video models are now smart enough to use those  
  references correctly (which may have improved significantly since the original build), then the shot-listing step becomes very fast — a scene with 8 shots could be   
  fully blocked out in under a minute — and the whole pipeline becomes viable.
